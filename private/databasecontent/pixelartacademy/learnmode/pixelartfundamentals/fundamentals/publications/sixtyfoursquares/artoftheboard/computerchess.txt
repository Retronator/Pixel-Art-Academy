The 8-bit Revolution
The 8-bit revolution
Humble beginnings
Chess has been on the minds of computer scientists from the very beginning, with even Alan Turing designing an algorithm for it in the early 1950s. Despite not being able to make it run on the computers of his time, by 1957, the first programs were able to play a full game.
In the 1970s, thanks to microprocessors, the marvel of playing chess against a computer moved from research labs and universities into living rooms.
Microchess in 1976 was the first software you could get your hands on and possibly the very first computer game ever to be sold independently (not bundled with the computer or console). Don't expect a fancy chessboard display, though—playing it was more like using a calculator.
This shortcoming was removed in 1978 with Microchess 1.5 on the TRS-80, and 2.0 for the Apple II, Commodore PET, and Atari 8-bit computers.
Another legend among chess programs, Sargon, joined the graphics club in 1978. At the West Coast Computer Faire, it won the first computer chess tournament for microcomputers, with a score of 5–0. A later port to Apple II had more elaborate sprites, just like Microchess.

On game consoles, chess with visuals could be played in the aptly named Video Chess for the Atari 2600 in 1979. The hardware was limited to 3 sprites per line (or 6 with clever programming), so to make the 8-wide chessboard a reality, the sprites had to be alternated between each row, leading to a recognizable striped look. The port Computer Chess on Atari 8-bit computers didn't have this limitation.
In Clear View
Practically every chess game features a 2D board view, as it offers the clearest look at the position. Visually, the pieces mimic the way chess appears in books and magazines.
With the typical resolution of early 1980s machines being around 320x200, most games featured chessboards with squares around 24 pixels in size (24×8=192, neatly fitting on the screen).
The resulting pieces were drawn to fit into those dimensions, leading to sprites around 20 pixels tall (to incorporate a bit of padding). 
Given the limited color palettes, pieces were most often black or white silhouettes displayed on colored squares. If not, as with Colossus Chess 4 and Cyrus on the TRS 80 Color Computer, you had to include an outline of the opposite color around the piece to make it stand out on squares of the same color as the piece.
If you were lucky enough to have one of the more expensive machines with superior color capabilities, elaborate shading was used to underscore the round form of the pieces. 

Some games, especially on the ZX Spectrum, chose to go smaller and fit pieces into 16-pixel squares for simplicity. And for ports that had to run on tiny screens of handheld consoles, this was a necessity.
The Third Dimension
Of course, if you wanted to really impress your friends—and we all did—the chess program had to dazzle the viewer by rendering a 3D board. Perhaps not used by the most serious players, this offered a more natural view we were used to from playing chess over the board.
A high view of the board was typically maintained to minimize overlap as the pieces were still drawn relatively from the side for maximum readability. The Chessmaster 2000, Cyrus II, and Sega Chess were some of the more realistic exceptions with a lower viewpoint.
With Sega Chess, they accommodate the strong perspective by using multiple sprites to represent pieces on the different ranks.
Yet other games tried to impress the players with arbitrary rotations of the board.
High resolution
Some computers had a relatively high resolution even in the 80s. The original Macintosh was a high-end machine optimized for work. The display was only 1-bit (black or white only), but at an unusually large resolution of 512×342. This allowed The Chessmaster 2000 to have much larger sprites than on typical machines and relied on dithering for shading. The pieces then used both black line art as well as a white outline to make the shapes read clearly against the checkerboard gray squares. 
A similar approach was used by Cyrus II on the Amstrad PCW, and by Sargon III.
Fantasy Worlds
We couldn't finish this article without mentioning a special sort of chessmen. Just as there are themed chess sets in the physical world, video games ran with the idea that warriors on the battlefield don't have to look that abstract.
The leader of the pack, Battle Chess, sold a quarter million copies by the mid 90s and made chess cool like no other. Its imaginative capture animations not only captured the pieces but the audience's attention, leading to many games following the formula, from Star Wars to The Terminator.
