Chess in Print
The Birth of a Standard
Our immediate impression of chess might be of two players hunched over a board, moving intricately carved pieces across the physical squares. The full picture would be missing how chess appeared on paper. It was as much a reflection of the actual activity as the inspiration for the chess pieces we imagine when we think of chess today.
The earliest European treatise on chess appears in the "Book of chess, dice and tables", commissioned by Alfonso X the Wise, the Spanish King of Castile, León and Galicia. Its meticulously illustrated manuscript pages present a rich cultural, social, and religious context with people of all ages and genders participating in the game. 
The Spanish chess player Luis Ramírez de Lucena published the first book on chess, with an imaginatively descriptive title "Repetition of Love and the Art of Playing Chess with 150 Games". Yes, he is the one the Lucena position is named after. Ironically, it does not appear in his book.
Around the same time, the mathematician Luca Pacioli wrote "On the Game of Chess". It is rumored that Leonardo da Vinci himself drew the chess pieces when the pair sheltered with the chess-loving Isabella d'Este, the Marchioness of Mantua, to whom the manuscript was dedicated. The pieces were drawn with the proportions of the golden ratio, an aesthetic admired by both Pacioli and Da Vinci.
A few hundred years later, drawn chess pieces regained the more figurative treatment, even as their physical counterparts still often fashioned more abstract shapes.
In the middle of the 19th century, the designs settled on the now-familiar balance between realism and simplification, spearheaded by the popular printing types of the time. 
Pawns retained their abstract treatment. The knights became the iconic horses. The two pointed shapes that originally represented elephant tusks were reinterpreted as many things, from a jester's hat in France (a fool) to a mitre—the pointed hat of a bishop—by the English. The rook or castle became a fortified tower, and the queen and king were differentiated by the use of a coronet vs. a crown.
The shapes were so recognizable that they inspired a new physical chess set—the Staunton chessmen—which has been the standard for chess pieces ever since.
Modern takes
The 6 chess pieces are now part of a common vocabulary and have been included in the very first Unicode standard published in 1991.
With the advent of digital publishing and internet communication, many fonts would give their own take on the 6 glyphs, some recreating the past, others pushing beyond the standard.
